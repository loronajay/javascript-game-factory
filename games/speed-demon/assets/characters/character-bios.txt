Ren “Prince” Takeda
Ren grew up close enough to power to understand what it could buy, but never close enough to actually possess any of it. His family’s connections introduced him to expensive cars, private garages, and racers most people only heard about through rumors. Instead of leaning on those connections, Ren became obsessed with proving that his driving could stand on its own.

He races with almost irritating discipline: clean launches, deliberate shifts, minimal mistakes. Older racers still see him as somebody’s kid, and that bothers him more than losing. Ren isn’t trying to become another lieutenant in somebody else’s organization. He wants a reputation that belongs entirely to him.



Mina “Zero” Park
Mina approaches racing like an engineering problem. Every launch has a target RPM, every shift has a preferred window, and every modification on her car exists because she can explain exactly what it does. She keeps detailed records of nearly every serious run she makes and has little patience for drivers who rely on bravado instead of preparation.

“Zero” came from her reputation for eliminating mistakes rather than taking risks. She rarely talks trash and almost never celebrates after winning. For Mina, beating somebody isn’t personal—it’s confirmation that her setup and execution were better. That attitude can make her seem cold, but underneath it is somebody who genuinely hates losing control of anything.



Valeria “Vee” Cruz
Valeria built her reputation racing cars that were regularly underestimated. She came up around informal street meets, cash races, and garages where keeping an old car alive required more creativity than money. She learned early that horsepower means very little when the driver beside you panics under pressure.

Vee is social, loud when she wants to be, and completely comfortable getting inside another driver’s head before a race. Unlike some of the campaign’s more calculated rivals, she genuinely enjoys the culture around racing—the music, arguments, late-night meets, rivalries, and stories people tell afterward. She races because winning is fun, but embarrassing somebody who underestimated her is even better.



Nico “Saint” Varela
Nobody remembers exactly who first started calling Nico “Saint,” but everyone agrees it was sarcastic. He has a long history of illegal races, questionable bets, broken agreements, and confrontations that somehow never seem to become serious enough to permanently shut him out of the scene.

Behind the reputation, however, Nico is an unusually controlled driver. He rarely gets emotional once a race starts and is exceptionally good at waiting for another driver to make the first mistake. He prefers psychological pressure to reckless aggression. Nico will smile, talk like the result is already decided, and then quietly punish every bad shift you make. Whether he actually likes anyone in the racing scene is difficult to tell.



Cole “Torque” Mercer
Cole never had the budget to simply buy a competitive car, so he learned how to build one. Years spent working in garages turned him into the kind of mechanic who can hear a problem before most drivers know anything is wrong. His cars usually look rougher than the competition, but almost nothing under the hood is there by accident.

He has little respect for people who buy horsepower without understanding it, which makes him especially abrasive around wealthy racers. Cole considers racing an extension of mechanical skill: the builder and driver are solving the same problem from different directions. He talks plenty of trash, but he’ll also stop what he’s doing to help somebody diagnose a broken car after the race is over.



Masaru Kuroda
Masaru Kuroda was involved with fast cars long before most of the current racing scene existed. What began decades ago as an interest in expensive imports and underground competition eventually became part of a much larger network of businesses, favors, and relationships. Racing is only one corner of Kuroda’s world now, but it remains one of the few things he appears to genuinely enjoy.

He rarely enters public races himself anymore. His presence is usually felt through other drivers, private events, exclusive garages, and invitations that arrive without much explanation. Kuroda speaks quietly and expects people to listen. He has little interest in intimidation for its own sake; the unsettling part is that he almost never needs it. If Kuroda personally challenges somebody, the race probably means far more than the prize.



Zaid “Mirage” Rahman
Zaid made his name on long highway runs where patience matters as much as raw acceleration. He prefers races that give a car enough room to reveal whether it was actually built properly. Short sprints bore him. Anybody can survive a few seconds of horsepower; maintaining control at sustained speed is what he considers real driving.

His calm demeanor and expensive-looking builds make people assume he comes from easy money, an assumption Zaid rarely bothers correcting. He enjoys cultivating a little mystery around himself. What competitors do know is that his cars are meticulously prepared and frighteningly stable at speed. By the time most drivers realize they are losing to Mirage, the gap has usually become impossible to recover.



Darius “Knox” Reed
Darius came out of the industrial side of the city, where empty access roads and warehouse districts became unofficial racing territory after midnight. His cars reflect that environment: aggressive setups, heavy power, and launches designed to immediately establish who controls the race.

Knox has a reputation for being intimidating, but people who actually know him describe somebody far more deliberate than his appearance suggests. He doesn’t start unnecessary problems and has little tolerance for people who bring violence or stupidity into racing. What he does demand is respect. If somebody calls him out, he expects them to show up. If they lose, he expects them to own it. To Knox, reputation is built almost entirely on whether your actions match your words.



Malakai “Mako” Taufua
Mako has been racing long enough to watch entire crews appear, dominate for a year, and disappear. He came through dockside and industrial racing circles where powerful cars and rough surfaces rewarded drivers who could keep their composure when everything started moving sideways.

He doesn’t chase attention and rarely participates in the social politics surrounding the scene. Younger racers sometimes mistake that quietness for disinterest until they line up beside him. Mako is brutally consistent and especially dangerous against impatient opponents. He doesn’t need a perfect race to beat you; he just needs you to make one mistake. Among veteran racers, getting a nod of approval from Mako carries considerably more weight than winning another argument at a car meet.



Nia “Switch” Carter
Nia is one of the people who quietly keeps the racing scene functioning. She knows which crews are fighting, which roads are being watched, who suddenly has a suspicious amount of horsepower, and where the next serious race is likely to happen. Years of organizing meets and communicating between groups earned her the name “Switch.”

None of that means she stays on the sidelines. Nia is a capable racer in her own right and particularly good at adapting when a race stops going according to plan. She reads opponents quickly and rarely allows herself to get rattled. Her network makes her valuable, but it also means she hears things other people would prefer stayed quiet. Becoming friends with Switch can open doors. Giving her a reason not to trust you can close a lot more.
